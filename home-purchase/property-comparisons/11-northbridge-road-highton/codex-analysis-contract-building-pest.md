## Executive Takeaway

This property is still attractive as a polished, compact Highton home, but the new documents materially reduce the confidence level from the earlier visual/floorplan assessment.

The biggest change is that the building inspection identifies **subsidence as a major defect** across the main building. That does not automatically mean "do not buy", but it does mean this should not be treated as a simple move-in-ready purchase unless a structural engineer and/or underpinning/restumping specialist confirms the extent, cause, likely repair path, and cost.

The second important change is that the Section 32 and service certificates are mostly clean but not completely clean. There is a registered covenant expiring on **1 June 2028**, multiple planning overlays, and Barwon Water notes that **sanitary plumbing work has not been finalised in accordance with VBA requirements**. Those points need conveyancer/plumber follow-up before signing anything unconditional.

My revised position: **conditional proceed only**. The home remains worth pursuing at the right price, but only with structural, drainage, plumbing, pest, and conveyancing checks resolved. I would not sign an unconditional offer based on the current packet.

## What Is Not Working

### 1. Major defect: subsidence

The building inspection marks **Major Defect found** and identifies the major defect as **subsidence** across the main building.

The inspector says laser level measurements were used and that the subfloor structure appears affected by movement of foundations. The report recommends:

- underpinner quote on repairs, if required
- stormwater drains to be grated so excess water flows away from the property
- structural engineer review to determine the required scope of works
- possible geotechnical engineer input if soil moisture changes are involved
- registered builder specialising in restumping/underpinning to carry out works if advised by an engineer

This is the single biggest risk in the whole document set. It directly affects future repair cost, insurability confidence, negotiation posture, and resale anxiety.

### 2. Drainage is not a side issue; it may be connected to the movement issue

The report repeatedly links movement/subsidence risk to soil moisture changes and stormwater management.

The minor defect for stormwater says all drains should have fixed grates so surface moisture can drain to the stormwater system as designed. The deck/subsidence-monitor item also recommends containing stormwater flows and ensuring pavements flow away from the building to reduce wetting/drying effects.

For this property, drainage is not merely cosmetic maintenance. It is part of the structural-risk story.

### 3. Decks are slightly out of level and should be monitored

The report identifies both entry and family decks as slightly out of level. The wording is less severe than the main subsidence defect, but it sits in the same movement/drainage cluster.

Given the home has a deck/view lifestyle proposition, this needs proper checking. A deck that is slightly out of level today could be harmless settlement, but it could also become an expensive maintenance item if movement continues.

### 4. Inspection access was materially limited

The report says the following were inaccessible:

- ceiling cavity
- part of the subfloor

It also notes:

- areas of skillion/flat roof had no access
- decking, concrete/paving, floor coverings, cabinetry, furniture, wall linings and other obstructions limited inspection
- subfloor was obscured due to poor clearance and obstructions
- less than 50% of the inspectable subfloor area was accessible
- undetected-defect risk is **Medium**

This is important because the major defect is already structural/foundation-related, and yet the subfloor inspection was incomplete. You should assume the risk band is wider than the report can prove.

### 5. This appears to be a building report, not a true pest report

The file name/user context describes a building and pest report, but the report text explicitly says: **"This is not a pest report."**

It does include a termite-management note, saying no evidence of a chemical installation was seen and that advice from a pest controller is recommended. But that is not the same as a dedicated timber pest inspection.

For decision-making, treat pest status as **not cleared** until a proper timber pest inspection is done or the vendor supplies one you can rely on.

### 6. No termite management system was evident

The report says there was no evidence of a chemical termite-management installation and recommends pest-controller advice as a short-term priority.

The report also says Geelong is considered a non-termite area in its context, but the broader terms still recommend regular timber pest inspections because termites are widespread across mainland Australia.

Practical interpretation: this is not a panic item, but it is not a green tick either.

### 7. Barwon Water says sanitary plumbing work has not been finalised

The Barwon Water certificate says no outstanding water/sewer notices are currently served, but then states:

> Barwon Water records indicate sanitary plumbing work on the subject property has not been finalised in accordance with Victorian Building Authority requirements.

This needs direct follow-up. It may be an admin/record-closure issue, or it may indicate plumbing work that requires finalisation/compliance documentation.

Because the building report also says its inspection is not a licensed plumbing report, this is a separate due-diligence item.

### 8. Covenant and overlays need legal interpretation

The title shows **Covenant AJ710516E**, expiring **1 June 2028**.

The Planning Property Report shows:

- General Residential Zone, Schedule 1
- Design and Development Overlay, Schedules 13 and 14
- Development Plan Overlay, Schedule 6
- Road Closure Overlay

These are not necessarily deal-breakers, especially if you are buying the home for current use rather than redevelopment. But they matter for future alterations, extensions, external changes, fencing/retaining-wall changes, and resale questions.

The covenant instrument itself should be reviewed by your conveyancer. The extracted contract text only shows the covenant exists; it does not clearly spell out all covenant obligations in readable form.

### 9. Contract terms push condition risk onto the purchaser

Several special conditions are buyer-unfriendly in the normal way vendor contracts often are:

- purchaser buys based on own inspections/enquiries
- purchaser cannot rely on marketing materials
- purchaser cannot claim compensation for building condition/state of repair
- purchaser accepts buildings/goods condition
- failure to comply with building regulations is not treated as a title defect
- deposit bond/bank guarantee not allowed unless specifically included
- default interest is amended to **16%**
- default notices/legal costs are specified
- each contract variation request costs **$88**

This is why the offer should be conditional. The contract is not giving you comfort on the exact things that now matter most: foundation movement, drainage, plumbing finalisation, pest status, and future compliance.

### 10. Loan/finance details appear blank in the contract particulars

The contract contains a section for loan condition details, but the extracted text shows lender, loan amount, and approval date as blank.

If you intend to make any offer subject to finance, that needs to be explicitly filled in and checked by your conveyancer before signing. Do not assume finance protection exists just because the standard contract has a loan-condition section.

## Negotiation Implications

The property should no longer be priced purely as a polished compact Highton home. The inspection packet gives a buyer a legitimate reason to require either:

1. a lower offer,
2. subject-to-building/engineering terms,
3. vendor-funded clarification/quotes, or
4. a walk-away right if structural/plumbing findings are not acceptable.

I would not use the previous "move-in-ready" feel as the anchor. The anchor should be:

- compact 3-bed layout,
- no dedicated study,
- no WIR/WIP,
- major subsidence defect,
- drainage works needed,
- sanitary plumbing record not finalised,
- pest not fully cleared,
- covenant/overlays to understand.

If the vendor is expecting a clean offer because the property presents beautifully, this document set gives you a rational basis to resist that.

## Recommended Conditions Before Offer Becomes Unconditional

Use conveyancer wording, but conceptually I would want these covered:

1. **Finance approval**, with lender, amount, and approval date completed.
2. **Building/structural due diligence**, specifically covering the reported subsidence and deck movement.
3. **Licensed drainage/plumbing inspection**, including stormwater pits/grates/falls and the Barwon Water sanitary plumbing finalisation issue.
4. **Timber pest inspection**, because the supplied report says it is not a pest report.
5. **Conveyancer approval of contract/Section 32**, including covenant AJ710516E, overlays, rates/taxes, services, and all special conditions.
6. **Right to withdraw or renegotiate** if structural, plumbing, drainage, pest, or legal findings are unsatisfactory.

## Questions to Send the Agent / Conveyancer

### Structural and building

- Has the vendor obtained any structural engineer report about the subsidence?
- Has any underpinning, restumping, levelling, or foundation work been completed?
- If yes, can they provide invoices, engineering drawings, warranties, and certificates?
- The inspection report references an underpinner quote image. Is that quote available in full?
- Are the deck and entry/family deck levels known to have moved since construction?
- Were the 2021 retaining wall/fence works connected to movement, drainage, boundary, or compliance issues?

### Drainage and plumbing

- What works are needed to add/fix grates to the stormwater pits/drains?
- Are all stormwater drains connected to the legal point of discharge?
- Has there been any history of water pooling, dampness, garage efflorescence, or water transfer near retaining walls?
- Why does Barwon Water say sanitary plumbing work has not been finalised in accordance with VBA requirements?
- Can the vendor provide plumbing compliance certificates or finalisation documents?

### Pest and termite

- Is there a separate timber pest report?
- Has any termite activity ever been detected?
- Has a termite-management system ever been installed?
- If not, can we arrange a pest inspection before the offer becomes unconditional?

### Legal/title/planning

- Please provide and explain Covenant AJ710516E.
- Does the covenant restrict extensions, external colours/materials, fences, retaining walls, landscaping, or use?
- What practical restrictions arise from DDO13, DDO14, DPO6, and RXO for this property?
- Are there any obligations relating to the nearby watercourse shown in property/planning maps?
- Are there any unregistered easements or shared service arrangements not visible on title?

### Contract mechanics

- If making an offer subject to finance, where exactly will lender, loan amount, and approval date be inserted?
- Can a building/structural/pest/plumbing condition be added?
- Can settlement date and any conditions be set in a way that does not trigger default/variation costs?

## Codex Takeaways

### What works

The property still has real appeal: Highton location, freestanding title, no owners corporation, connected services, modern build records, low-maintenance land size, strong presentation, good roof/subfloor observations where visible, and no safety hazard reported.

### What does not work

The major defect is serious enough that this cannot be treated as a normal cosmetic-maintenance property. Subsidence plus drainage issues plus limited subfloor access is a meaningful risk cluster. The Barwon Water sanitary plumbing note adds another non-cosmetic issue. The supplied report also does not clear pest risk.

### My bottom line

I would keep the property alive, but only as a **conditional, price-disciplined candidate**. The right path is not "fall in love and offer fast"; it is "use the inspection report to force clarity."

Before proceeding, get:

- structural engineer view,
- underpinner/restumping quote if engineer recommends,
- drainage/plumbing inspection,
- pest inspection,
- conveyancer summary of covenant/overlays/contract conditions,
- written explanation and closeout path for the Barwon Water sanitary plumbing issue.

If those come back benign and repair costs are low/manageable, this remains a viable Highton option. If the engineer sees ongoing movement, significant underpinning need, or unresolved drainage/foundation interaction, I would walk away unless the price discount is large enough to compensate for cost, stress, and resale risk.
