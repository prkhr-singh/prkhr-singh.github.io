[The Next Jobs]({{ '/ai/the-next-jobs/' | relative_url }}) asked what work looks like after AI. This one asks the political version of the same question. Every earlier revolution in technology—writing, printing, industry—was followed by a new political order. So what follows AI?

The working theory that came out of the history is simple. People gained a voice in government whenever rulers came to need something they could withhold: their taxes, their labour, their service as soldiers. AI weakens all three. If that is right, the question is no longer only how to protect democracy, but where ordinary people's leverage will come from next.

## What I made

A 100-slide perspective deck in six parts:

- **How people gained a voice**, from the first taxable grain harvests through Athens' rowers, medieval parliaments, printing, industrial cities and the waves of democracy after 1945;
- **Why democracy stalled**: rights deepened and sovereignty was pooled for fifty years, then trust, turnout and the international order went into retreat, while the basic machinery of representation has barely changed since 1920;
- **The new powers**: companies that now rival states in reach and out-innovate them in governance, and AI weakening the levers citizens have held over their rulers;
- **Experiments already running**, from citizens' assemblies chosen by lot and digital government to public wealth funds and AI-assisted consultation;
- **First principles**: what a state is actually for, which of those functions still need territory, and what it would mean for every citizen to hold one share in the state that can never be sold; and
- **A proposal** with eight components, ten predictions and the main risk: a comfortable autocracy in which people are provided for but no longer needed.

## Where AI helped

As with The Next Jobs, I gave AI a brief and asked it to reason from history and first principles before projecting forward. The brief began with six beliefs of mine, and the instruction was not to take any of them on trust. The deck reports the verdict on each: three were supported outright and three were qualified in ways that changed the conclusion.

That was the most useful part of the exercise. A model that is asked to test your assumptions, rather than illustrate them, is a better thinking partner than one asked to agree.

## What changed for me

I started with a vague sense that democracy had stopped evolving. I ended with a sharper version of the problem: the question is less about which values to defend and more about design, and about making sure people keep a share in decisions even when they are no longer needed as workers, taxpayers or soldiers.
